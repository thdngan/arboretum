Axions were first proposed to solve the strong CP problem in quantum chromodynamics (QCD). In simple terms, QCD, the theory describing the [[strong interactions|strong nuclear force]], contains a term that should violate charge-parity symmetry in the interactions of [[quarks]] and [[gluons]], and nothing in the theory forces its coefficient to be small. Measurements of the [[neutrons|neutron]] electric dipole moment say it is smaller than about 10⁻¹⁰, which is a very specific kind of nothing, and nobody knows why. Axions are one way out: promote that coefficient to a field and it relaxes to zero by itself instead of having to be set by hand.

Axions might be more than a neat mathematical fix though. They could also be the long-sought answer to dark matter. Dark matter doesn’t interact electromagnetically at all, so it neither emits nor absorbs light, and only reveals itself through [[gravity]]. An axion would be neutral, extremely light and [[weak interactions|weakly interacting]] enough that an enormous invisible field of them could fill the universe unnoticed, perhaps the kind of thing dark matter needs to be?

All of which also makes them almost impossible to detect, but researchers are trying. The main handle is that an axion passing through a strong magnetic field should convert into a [[photons|photon]]. ADMX (Axion Dark Matter Experiment) runs a tunable microwave cavity inside a superconducting magnet and sweeps the resonant frequency looking for exactly that, one candidate axion mass at a time, which is slow.
