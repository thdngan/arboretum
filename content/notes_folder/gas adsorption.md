Adsorption is gas molecules sticking to a surface. Absorption is a gas going into the bulk of a material. The difference is one letter and well... if one's not a native English speaker, it can get confusing, because adsorption scales with surface area while absorption scales with volume. That is why adsorbents are made porous: activated carbon runs from several hundred to a few thousand m² per gram, almost all of it internal pore surface.

Physisorption holds molecules by van der Waals forces, at binding energies of a few tens of kJ/mol. It is weak, unfussy about what it binds, reversible, and it falls away quickly with temperature, so a swing in pressure or temperature releases whatever was captured. Chemisorption forms a real chemical bond instead, an order of magnitude stronger, specific to both the surface and the adsorbate, and often not reversible without wrecking something.

The distinction decides what a surface is good for. Storage and separation want physisorption, because whatever goes on has to come back off. Catalysis wants chemisorption, because the point is to weaken a bond in the adsorbed molecule so that it reacts over a lower barrier than it would in the gas phase. The standard measurement is an isotherm, the amount adsorbed plotted against pressure at fixed temperature. The Langmuir model assumes a single layer on identical sites and saturates.
