A baryon is a [[hadrons|hadron]] made of 3 [[quarks]] bound by the [[strong interactions|strong force]]. [[protons|Protons]] and [[neutrons]] are the only 2 stable enough to build anything out of. Other baryons exist, but they're unstable. Replace one or more of the up or down quarks with a heavier strange quark and you get a family called hyperons, named with Greek letters: Lambda (Λ), Sigma (Σ), Xi (Ξ) and Omega (Ω). Most of them decay in around 10⁻¹⁰ seconds, so they only show up briefly in particle accelerators and cosmic ray showers, never in ordinary matter.

Baryon number is conserved in every interaction measured so far, which is what stops a proton decaying into lighter things. The universe has a net baryon number though, and nothing in the Standard Model accounts for it, since equal amounts of matter and antimatter would have annihilated into radiation and left nothing behind.

Each quark has spin 1/2, and three of them always add up to a half-integer, so baryons are [[fermions]]. That means the Pauli exclusion principle applies: no 2 identical baryons can occupy the same state. Squeeze enough neutrons together and they are forced into higher and higher energy levels, which creates an outward push called degeneracy pressure. In a neutron star, this pressure (not heat and not any fusion or chemistry) is what holds the star up against its own gravity. Above roughly 2 solar masses, gravity wins and the star collapses into a black hole.

Ordinary (baryonic) matter is also a small part of the universe. It makes up only about 5% of the universe's total energy content. Dark matter, which has mass but gives off no light, makes up about 27%, and dark energy makes up the remaining 68%. Every galaxy we can see sits inside a much larger, invisible cloud of dark matter, so everything we observe directly is only a small fraction of what's actually there.
