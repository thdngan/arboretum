A boson is any particle with integer spin. That one fact sets everything else. The wavefunction of a set of identical bosons is symmetric under exchange, so the Pauli exclusion principle does not apply and any number of them can sit in the same state. Half-integer spin gives the antisymmetric case and exclusion instead, which is why [[fermions]] make up matter and bosons do not.

Letting a macroscopic number of particles pile into one state is what makes a laser work, and a Bose-Einstein condensate is the same thing in a cloud of cold atoms.

The gauge bosons carry the forces, one set for each symmetry of the Standard Model. [[photons|The photon]] carries electromagnetism and is massless. The eight [[gluons]] carry the [[strong interactions|strong force]], also massless but confined. The W± and Z⁰ carry the [[weak interactions|weak force]] at 80.4 and 91.2 GeV, and that mass is the whole reason the weak force stops at around 10⁻¹⁸ m. The [[gravitons|graviton]] would be the fourth, if gravity is quantised the way the others are.

The Higgs is the odd one, spin 0 rather than spin 1. It does not carry a force between particles. It is the excitation of the field whose nonzero vacuum value is what gives the W, the Z and the fermions their masses.

Spin adds, so composite objects can be bosons too. A [[mesons|meson]] is a quark and an antiquark whose half-integer spins sum to an integer, and a helium-4 nucleus does the same with two protons and two neutrons, which is why helium-4 goes superfluid at 2.2 K while helium-3 needs a different mechanism and a temperature a thousand times lower. Proposed particles outside the Standard Model are often bosons as well, including [[axions]] and [[majorons]].
