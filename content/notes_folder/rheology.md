Rheology is how materials deform and flow under stress, mostly the ones that do not sit neatly on either side of the solid/liquid line.

A Newtonian fluid has a viscosity that does not care how hard you shear it, so stress stays proportional to strain rate. Water and air behave this way and most other things do not. Shear-thinning materials lose viscosity as the shear rate climbs, which is why paint spreads under a brush and then stops running the moment the brush leaves. Shear-thickening materials stiffen under fast shear instead, because the particles jam before they have time to rearrange. Others have a yield stress and will not flow at all until it is exceeded, which is the difference between toothpaste and honey.

Time matters as much as rate. A viscoelastic material stores part of a deformation elastically and loses the rest to flow, and which of the two you see depends on how long you watch relative to its relaxation time. That ratio is the Deborah number (e.g.? how glacier ice shatters under a hammer and flows under its own weight over a decade, the mantle carries seismic shear waves over seconds while convecting over millions of years)
