A positron is the [[electrons|electron]]'s antiparticle. It has the same mass and spin, and the charge is reversed along with every other additive quantum number.

It does not last in ordinary matter. A positron slows down, meets an electron, and the two annihilate into a pair of [[photons]] at 511 keV. They come out back to back, since the pair is nearly at rest by then and the momentum has to cancel. In a solid the whole sequence takes a few hundred picoseconds, which is why no free antimatter is lying around.

Beta-plus decay produces them, when a [[protons|proton]] in a proton-rich nucleus converts to a [[neutrons|neutron]] and emits a positron and a [[neutrinos|neutrino]]. PET imaging is built on the back-to-back geometry: a detector ring catches both photons in coincidence and that fixes the annihilation to a line rather than a point. The positron travels a millimetre or two before it annihilates, so that distance is the resolution limit.
