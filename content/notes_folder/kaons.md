A kaon is a [[mesons|meson]] containing a strange [[quarks|quark]]. That quark is why kaons last as long as they do. The [[strong interactions|strong force]] conserves strangeness, so a kaon cannot decay strongly into lighter [[pions]] and has to wait for the [[weak interactions|weak force]], which is slower by many orders of magnitude.

The neutral ones are the interesting case. K⁰ and its antiparticle turn into each other through a second-order weak process, so neither has a definite lifetime. What propagates instead are two mixtures of the pair, and they behave nothing alike: one decays in 90 ps and the other in 51 ns, a factor of about 600. A beam becomes almost pure long-lived kaon within a short distance of where it was made.

If CP were an exact symmetry the long-lived state could not decay to two pions at all. It does, in about two of every thousand decays. That was the first time anyone saw matter and antimatter behave differently, and it is one of the conditions any account of why the universe has more matter than antimatter has to meet.
