A bottom-simulating reflector is a seismic reflection that runs parallel to the seafloor instead of following the sediment bedding, and cuts across that bedding wherever the two disagree. Bedding records how sediment was laid down. A BSR records where a temperature and pressure condition is currently met, and that surface tracks the shape of the seabed above it.

The condition is the base of the gas hydrate stability zone. Above it, methane and water are locked into a [[clathrate|hydrate]] that cements the sediment and raises its seismic velocity. Below it, hydrate cannot form and free gas collects instead, which drops the velocity sharply. The reflection comes off that contrast, and because velocity falls across the boundary rather than rising, a BSR has reversed polarity compared to the seafloor reflection. Polarity is how you tell one from an ordinary strong bedding reflector.

Its depth is a measurement rather than just an observation. Fix the pressure from the water depth, assume a pore water composition, and the depth of the BSR tells you the temperature at that depth, which gives you the geothermal gradient through the sediment column without drilling anything. It also moves. Warm the bottom water or drop the sea level and the stability boundary shifts, hydrate at the old boundary dissociates, and the gas released raises pore pressure in sediment that the hydrate had been cementing. That is the proposed mechanism behind some submarine landslides, and it is why hydrate stability keeps coming up in arguments about past climate.
