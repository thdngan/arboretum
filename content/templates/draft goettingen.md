In the spring and summer of 2025, I was spending my days simulating the chaotic [[Mars hydrogen escape|escape of hydrogen atoms]] from the Martian atmosphere. Meanwhile, my actual physical life felt completely stationary.

I was doing a 5-month internship at the Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research in Göttingen. On paper, it was the absolute dream. 18-year-old me, growing up in a developing country with basically no space industry, would never have believed I would end up doing... \**gestures vaguely*\* all this, \**more vague gestures*\*, in places like this. I was working on cool projects, looking at Mars, Titan, Enceladus, and getting paid to do it.

But the reality of living abroad is rarely as romantic as the high school version of yourself imagines it will be.

Most of my life as an international (wannabe) researcher consisted of sitting in my apartment on gloomy, freezing Saturdays. I have a diary entry from late May that perfectly sums up the vibe. I had this grand plan to wake up early, take the train to Berlin, have lunch with a friend before his flight, and wander around the city. Instead, I stayed up until 4 AM reading novels and slept right through my alarm.

I spent that entire weekend eating lazy meals where I just tossed fried eggs, tofu, random vegetables, and Korean spicy noodle sauce into a single bowl because I hated doing dishes. I even realized I had been living in Germany for 3 months and had not drank a single drop of German beer, mostly because I left my bottle opener in a drawer at the institute and was too lazy to go get it.

I took solo weekend trips to places like Hamburg, fully expecting to feel like the mysterious, independent protagonist of an indie movie. I thought I would thrive in that quiet environment. I am a massive introvert, after all. But walking around these beautiful, unfamiliar cities by myself, I mostly just felt bored. When you see something amazing, you instinctively want to turn around and point it out to someone. When there is no one there, the experience just falls a bit flat.

It made me realize two things. Either I am way more social than I ever thought I was, or I just have not unlocked the necessary character development to truly enjoy absolute solitude.

This sense of isolation started to bleed into a larger existential crisis. The nomadic reality of academia was catching up with me. When you are younger, the idea of moving countries every few years for research projects sounds like an adventure. When you are actually doing it, it is just exhausting.

It is the constant, draining friction of holding a weak passport and fighting through visa paperwork every single time you need to cross a border (got me thinking about the concept of [[geographical luck|geographical luck]] a lot more often than before). It is dragging 30 kilograms of luggage through metro stations that do not have escalators because you cannot afford a taxi. It is getting yelled at with racial slurs ("fuck you China girl") on a busy Paris street in broad daylight just trying to buy a bánh mì with your savings (damn, the irony of being called Chinese as a Vietnamese buying Vietnamese food in a Vietnamese restaurant). It is the heavy, quiet guilt that eats you up every time you go back home and notice your parents have aged another year.
 
I loved the science. I knew I still wanted a PhD. But I was dreading the next 5 to 10 years of uprooting my life over and over again.

And right in the middle of all this mental chaos, a researcher at the institute offered me a PhD project.

It was a golden ticket. It meant stable funding, incredible prestige, and a guaranteed spot at a top-tier research facility. It was exactly the kind of opportunity you are supposed to take without thinking twice.

I thought about it for a long time. And then I said no.

Part of the reason was academic. The project was interesting, but it just did not align perfectly with my actual research passions. But the bigger reason was entirely personal. I simply could not picture myself spending 3 more years in Germany.

Science is collaborative, but life outside the lab matters too. I missed France (surprise!), where I actually have a support system and friends I can rely on. I realized that if I am going to uproot my life and endure the brutal academic grind for a PhD, I need to be in a place where I can genuinely connect with the people around me. I need to be in a country where I speak the language comfortably, or at least an English-speaking country where building a community does not feel like scaling a brick wall. Vietnam would obviously be the easiest place for that, but returning home would mean giving up the planetary science dream entirely.

Turning down a sure thing in a notoriously brutal academic job market felt terrifying. But I guess... walking away from that offer also felt... sort of, kind of, empowering?

It was the first time I made a major career decision based on what I actually wanted for my day-to-day life, rather than just doing what would look best on a CV. I mean, of course it is okay to draw boundaries around your own happiness. The science will always be there, but you have to actually survive the life you build around it. For now, I like to believe that I made the right decision.

# five months in a city where i knew no one

_Oct 10, 2025 · writings · personal_

---

I'm going to try something different with this post. My older writing here is mostly about topics — clathrates, lithium, whatever I was reading that month. Impersonal. Safely behind the science. This one is going to be more about what it actually felt like to be doing any of it.

So: Göttingen, Germany. January to June 2025, more or less. A small university city in Lower Saxony that I had never heard of before my supervisor sent me there for an internship at the Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research. I was there to model hydrogen escape from Mars — that part, I've written about [[Hydrogen diffusion and thermal escape in the Martian thermosphere|elsewhere]]. This part is about the other stuff.

---

The thing nobody tells you about being an introvert is that loneliness sneaks up on you differently than it does for extroverts. You can go days without speaking to anyone and feel fine, maybe even relieved. And then one random afternoon you're sitting in your apartment eating fried eggs with soy sauce over pasta (no rice cooker, this is what we do now) and you suddenly notice that you haven't had a real conversation in two weeks, and the feeling hits all at once like a delayed bus.

I went to Hamburg alone. Bremen alone. I had a whole weekend in Berlin planned, and I woke up too late because I'd been up until 3am reading fanfiction, which is maybe the most honest thing I can tell you about that period of my life. I watched the missed train time pass on my phone and thought: _I'm not even that sad about it._ And then I wasn't sure whether that meant I was doing okay, or whether I'd just gotten so used to being by myself that I'd lost the ability to want things.

I think what I learned — slowly, in the way you only learn things when you're bored enough to actually sit with them — is that I'm more of a social person than I thought I was. I always identified as a hardcore introvert. Recharge alone, all that. And that's still true. But there's a difference between choosing solitude and having solitude chosen for you, and I'd never really sat with the second kind before.

---

The science was going well, by the way. My supervisor was kind and brilliant and treated me like a real researcher, which was a new experience and one I didn't fully appreciate until I later heard about other people's supervisors. The work was interesting — genuinely interesting, not interesting-because-I-have-to-make-it-interesting. I was writing actual Fortran (extremely unglamorous, I know) and running simulations on Mars and going to seminars about Titan and arguing with papers about exobase temperatures. Eighteen-year-old me would have been absolutely unhinged with excitement about this.

But also: I spent a lot of time alone in a mid-sized German city where I didn't really have people to call when I didn't feel like going home to an empty apartment.

---

Toward the end of the internship, a well-known researcher at the institute — a colleague of my supervisor — offered me a PhD position. I said no.

I want to be honest about why, because I've thought about it a lot. The project itself was interesting enough. The institute is genuinely world-class. And saying no to a funded PhD position when you're a master's student from a country with no space industry and a scholarship-or-nothing financial background is... not an easy thing to explain to people.

The main reason was research fit. The project would have taken me in a direction I find intellectually interesting but not personally compelling in the way I'd need it to be for three-plus years of deep commitment. I have a fairly clear sense of what I want to work on — it's adjacent, but not that.

But the other reason, the one I am slightly embarrassed to admit but will anyway: I knew I wouldn't be happy. Not _couldn't_ be happy — I'm not catastrophizing — but that I'd be spending three years in a place where I didn't have friends, in a language I don't speak, in a country that is beautiful and well-organized and where I always felt slightly alien. I have more friends in France. I've built something there. And if I'm going somewhere completely new for a PhD, I'd rather it be an English-speaking country where I have a real shot at connecting with people beyond the purely professional.

That probably sounds soft, and maybe it is. But I watched myself go a little bit gray during those five months, and I think I know now that research alone isn't enough to sustain me. I need people too. I'm still figuring out what that means for the rest of my career.

---

I also had some experiences during this period that I wrote about anonymously online — about housing nightmares, about the visa paperwork that never ends, about being yelled at on the street for not giving money to someone who assumed I was Chinese, about watching my parents age in photos on my phone while I'm thousands of kilometers away trying to justify taking up space at institutions that weren't built for people like me.

I don't have a neat conclusion for any of that. I was having a crisis, the normal kind, the kind a lot of people my age are quietly having. The academic job market is brutal. My passport is weak. My field is niche. These are real constraints and not things I can think-positive my way out of.

But I'm still here, still doing the research, still excited about Mars. And the paper I worked on that semester — really worked on, spent months on, argued with the literature about — is currently under review at a journal. That matters to me. That's mine.

The loneliness was real. So was the work. Both of those things get to be true.

# 400 grams per second: how Mars quietly loses its water

_Jul 31, 2025 · planetary-science · writings_

---

There's a particular kind of freedom that comes with being completely unknown somewhere. No social obligations, no history, no one who knew you before. You can reinvent yourself, in theory. In practice, what mostly happens is that you eat the same 3 meals on rotation and develop strong opinions about which supermarket has the better yogurt.

I spent 5 months in Göttingen, Germany  (January to June 2025) doing an internship as part of my master's program. The science is written up [[Mars hydrogen escape|elsewhere]], this is the other part of the story.

---

Göttingen is a beautiful, quiet university town. It has a famous tradition: students who pass their doctorate are dragged to the market square and kiss the cheek of a bronze goose-girl fountain. There are plaques for Nobel laureates everywhere, because an absurd number of them passed through. The cafes are good. The transit works. Apparently it snowed in February in a way that was genuinely pretty before becoming genuinely inconvenient (coming from the tropics, I actually don't mind...).

I went there knowing no one, which I thought I was fine with. I am, after all, an introvert. I like being alone. And for a while that was true. The first few weeks have a honeymoon quality, everything is new, even a grocery run is a small adventure in a foreign language.

But solitude and isolation are different things, and I came to understand the difference that semester more clearly than I had before. Solitude is what you choose. Isolation is what accumulates when you realize you haven't had a real conversation in longer than you'd like to admit, and that there's no one to call when you don't feel like going home to an empty apartment.

I went to Hamburg alone and liked it. I got stranded near Schlüchtern alone and liked it less. I planned a whole trip to Berlin on a Saturday, stayed up too late the night before, and missed my train. I sat with the missed departure time on my phone for a while and noticed I wasn't particularly devastated, and then I wasn't sure if that was equanimity or just the gradual blunting of wanting things. In the end, I never made it to Berlin. It was fine.

---

What I learned, or started to learn, is that I am more social than I had accounted for. This was genuinely surprising to me. I had always understood myself as someone who finds people draining, who needs significant alone time to function, who prefers a quiet room to a crowded one. All of that is still true. But there is a version of aloneness that is chosen and energizing, and a version that is circumstantial and quietly corrosive, and I had only really experienced the first kind before.

I guess it's the kind of thing that's hard to know about yourself until the conditions force it.

---

The research was good. My supervisor was generous with his time and treated my ideas as worth engaging with, which matters more than it sounds. The work (building a diffusion module to model hydrogen escape from Mars, running it on a Martian general circulation model, comparing the results against observations) was the kind of project that is hard in ways that feel worthwhile. I was doing real science on a real problem, and the results, I think, are real contributions.

Toward the end, a senior researcher at the institute offered me a PhD position. I thought about it for a long time. And then I said no.

Part of the reason was academic. The project was interesting, but it just did not align perfectly with my actual research passions. But the bigger reason was entirely personal. I simply could not picture myself spending 3 more years in Germany. Research is not sufficient by itself. I need other things too, including people, including some sense of belonging to a place. That's not a small consideration.

If I do a PhD, and I still want to, I think it needs to be somewhere where I have some foothold. France, where I've built friendships. Or an English-speaking country, where I'd at least have the language as a bridge. I'm aware this constrains my options. I've decided I'm okay with that.

---

That semester also clarified some things I'd been vaguely aware of but hadn't looked at directly. The particular strain of being a foreign student on a scholarship in Europe: the visa paperwork that treats you as a suspect until proven otherwise, the cost of cities on a stipend that wasn't designed to cover them, the specific exhaustion of navigating institutions that weren't built with people like you in mind. None of that is new information, exactly. But living it continuously for months, away from anyone who knew me, made it harder to set aside.

I'm not sure what to do with most of it. Some of it is systemic and I can't fix it. Some of it I can navigate more wisely now that I know the terrain. Some of it I just have to carry.

---

The paper that came out of that internship is currently under review. I'm proud of it, in the way you're proud of something you struggled with and eventually made work. The 5 months were hard in specific ways I hadn't expected. Both of those things are true, and neither cancels the other out.

I'm writing this partly because I want to remember it accurately — not just the work, but what it cost and what it gave back.
